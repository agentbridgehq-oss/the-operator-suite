PATH 01

Sell an AI playbook

You already know one job well enough to write it down. A stranger will pay to skip the guessing.

Open high-quality PDF

Who this is for
Someone who can explain a single result — a week of content, a client intake, a job application pack — better than a blank chat window.
First sale
A $19–$47 download bought by a person who is not you.
Time to first
7–21 days if you already know the job
Cash window
$19–$47 first offer
Tools
Google Docs or Word, Stripe Payment Link or Stan Store, one simple page, a second email to test-buy.
Leave this alone
A 200-page ‘everything AI’ book, fifteen products, or waiting for a course platform.

What this actually is

A playbook is not a memoir about AI. It is a job, written so a tired person can finish it without inventing the steps. The buyer is not shopping for inspiration. They are shopping for a sequence they can run this week.

The products that sell in this category are narrow. ‘How to use ChatGPT’ sits in a pile of free posts. ‘A 10-day content system for a one-person landscaping company’ is a product. The more specific the job, the less you compete with free generic advice.

You are allowed to use AI to draft. You are not allowed to ship the draft. The money is in the rewrite: your examples, your order of operations, your ‘do this next’ at the end of every chapter. If a chapter does not end with a next action, it is an essay. Cut it or finish it.

Who this is for — and who should skip it

This path fits if you have done a job more than three times and can name what went wrong the first time. That scar tissue is the product. People pay for the mistakes you already made.

Skip this path if you have never finished a result for anyone, including yourself. Write the playbook after you have a result, not as a way to avoid getting one. If you need cash this week, use a service path first, then turn the notes into this playbook.

The offer, named in one sentence

Write this sentence before you outline: ‘I help [specific person] get [named result] in [time] using [named tools], even if [honest objection].’ If you cannot fill the brackets, you do not have an offer yet.

Examples that work: a solo accountant who needs a month-end close checklist in Claude; a realtor who needs listing descriptions without sounding like a brochure; a tradesperson who wants five Google-ready posts a week without sitting on a phone for two hours.

Price the result, not the page count. First playbooks sell at $19–$47. Raise to $67–$97 when the file has templates, scripts, and a 14-day plan. Do not start at $297. You have no proof yet.

The first 72 hours

Hour 1–3: pick the buyer and the result. Write the one-sentence offer. If you have two ideas, pick the one you can finish without research you have not done.

Hour 4–8: outline 8–15 chapters. Each chapter title is a verb plus an object: ‘Write the three hooks,’ ‘Set the checkout,’ ‘Send the ten messages.’ Put a next action under each.

Day 2: draft ugly. Use AI for a first pass if you want, then rewrite every paragraph in the voice you would use to a friend at a kitchen table. Cut hype. Keep the steps.

Day 3: make the checkout. One product, one price, one file. Buy it yourself from a second email. If the download fails, nothing else matters. Then send ten messages to people who already know you. That is the whole 72 hours.

  • One buyer, one result, one sentence
  • 8–15 chapters that each end with a next action
  • Ugly complete draft, then a human rewrite
  • Working checkout, self-purchase, ten asks

The path, step by step

Step 1. Name the job on a sticky note. If it takes more than one line, it is two products. Split it.

Step 2. Collect the raw material you already have: old emails, checklists, prompts that worked, screenshots of a finished result. This is faster than inventing chapters from a blank page.

Step 3. Outline for a reader who is tired. First chapter: what ‘done’ looks like. Last chapter: what they do next week. Middle chapters are the work.

Step 4. Draft. Do not format yet. Do not design a cover. Words first.

Step 5. Rewrite for voice. Read a page out loud. If you would not say it, delete it.

Step 6. Add the working files: a template, a prompt block, a checklist. Buyers pay more when they can copy, not just read.

Step 7. Export a clean PDF. Number the chapters. Put your email on the last page for questions you actually want.

Step 8. Checkout. Stripe Payment Link or Stan. Title matches the one-sentence offer.

Step 9. Test-buy. Different browser, different email. Confirm the file arrives and opens.

Step 10. Ten personal messages. Not a broadcast. ‘I wrote this for [job]. $19. If it is useful: [link]. If not, ignore.’

Step 11. After the first stranger sale, ask what they used. That sentence becomes your next ten posts.

Step 12. Only then add a second product or a $9 membership. Not before.

Copy you can actually send

DM: ‘I put a short playbook live for [job]. One file, $19, instant download. If that is useful: [link]. If not, no need to reply.’

Post: ‘Blank chat is expensive. I wrote the sequence I actually use for [result]. $19. Link in bio.’

Listing title: ‘[Job] in [time] — a playbook for [person], not another AI dump.’

Week-by-week if you have a job

Week 1: outline and ugly draft. No public launch.

Week 2: rewrite, templates, checkout, ten asks. First sale is allowed to be someone you know.

Week 3: three posts or emails that teach one chapter. Soft link.

Week 4: fix whatever the first buyers tripped on. Do not write book two.

Mistakes that kill this path

Writing for ‘entrepreneurs.’ That is not a buyer. Writing 80 pages of theory. Shipping a ChatGPT paste. Hiding the price. Launching with five bonuses and no core file. Waiting until it feels ready.

The playbook is ready when a stranger can finish chapter one without you in the room. That is the bar. Everything else is decoration.

What a skill pack is

A skill pack is a working stack: instructions the model follows, examples of good output, and a way to install it in under ten minutes. It is not a PDF of prompts. It is closer to a small appliance. Plug it in, get the job.

The buyer has already decided AI is useful. Their pain is the blank window. Your job is to replace that window with a named job: weekly content, invoice follow-up, meeting notes into tasks, a listing description, a study plan.

If they cannot tell from the title what file they get and what job it does, they will not buy. ‘Claude Power Pack’ is a mood. ‘Client follow-up skill for solo bookkeepers’ is a product.

The first 72 hours

Pick one job you have done in a chat more than twice. Open a new project. Write the system instructions as if you will never be in the room: role, inputs they must paste, output format, quality checks, what to ask if information is missing.

Run it on a real example. Save the output as the sample. Write a one-page install: where to click, what to paste, how to test. Zip it. Price $19. Record a 20-second screen: blank chat versus the skill.

Put the video and the checkout in one place. Ask five people who already use Claude or ChatGPT. Do not explain the catalog of other ideas you have.

  • One job, written as system instructions
  • One real example output in the zip
  • One-page install a tired person can follow
  • Twenty-second screen recording
  • Five asks, not fifty features

The path, step by step

Step 1. Watch how the buyer actually works. If they live in email, do not build a Notion skill.

Step 2. Write the skill so the first message from the user is just the raw material. The skill should not need a prompt engineer on their side.

Step 3. Add failure modes: ‘If the invoice amount is missing, ask once, then stop.’ Silent guessing is how people lose trust.

Step 4. Version the file. v1 is allowed to be narrow. You will ship v1.1 after the first buyer.

Step 5. Package: README, skill or instructions file, sample input, sample output. That is enough.

Step 6. Checkout title is the job. Thumbnail is the screen recording still.

Step 7. Sell where those users already complain: X threads, Reddit help posts, Discord. Help first. Link in profile.

Step 8. After purchase, a three-line email: install, test with the sample, reply if it breaks. That reply is your product roadmap.

Copy

Post: ‘I wasted two hours prompting this. The skill does it in one paste. $19.’

Reply under other people’s Claude threads: show the result, not the pitch. Profile holds the link.

Listing: ‘Install in 10 minutes. Built for [job]. Not a prompt dump.’

Week-by-week

Day 1–2: write and test the skill on a real job. Day 3: package and checkout. Day 4–7: five asks plus one screen recording posted once a day.

Week 2: fix whatever the first buyer could not install. Ship v1.1. Do not add a second skill until this one has a stranger sale.

Mistakes

Shipping prompts without install steps. Promising it works on every model. Bundling 40 jobs so none of them are good. Using other people’s copyrighted prompt packs as your file.

Niche prompt stacks

Twenty-five prompts that finish a job beat a thousand that sound clever.

First sale

Time to first
5–14 days
Cash window
$12–$29
Tools
A PDF or Notion doc, three before/after examples, Stripe or Etsy or Stan.

Generic ‘1,000 ChatGPT prompts.’ That market is dead and noisy.

Why niche wins

Generic prompt packs are free on every platform. A stack for ‘pediatric clinic SMS that stays compliant-sounding and human’ is not. You are selling vocabulary and constraints the general pack does not have.

Every prompt in the stack needs three parts: inputs (what they paste), output format (what they get), and a done check (how they know it is usable). Without those, it is a toy.

The first 72 hours

List the ten repeating writing jobs in that niche. Pick the five that happen every week. Write five prompts each, or write 25 that cover a month of work.

Run all 25 on real or realistic inputs. Keep the three best before/after pairs as proof. Write a one-page SOP: which prompt when.

Price $19. Title includes the niche word they would search. Ask in the Facebook group or forum you already belong to — helpfully, once.

The path, step by step

Step 1. Sit with a practitioner, even if it is you. Write the jobs they hate writing.

Step 2. For each job, write the prompt as a form: ‘Paste [x]. I will return [y] in [format]. I will not [constraint].’

Step 3. Add a second pass prompt: ‘Tighten this. Keep the facts. Cut the fluff.’ Bundles sell when there is a polish step.

Step 4. Show three before/after. Blur names. Keep the texture of real work.

Step 5. Update when the model changes. A dated ‘tested on [month year]’ line builds trust.

Step 6. Sell on your site first. Marketplace second if the titles match search.

Copy you can send

Listing: ‘25 prompts for [niche job] — inputs, output format, done check. Tested this month.’

Post: ‘Generic prompt packs are free. This one is for [person] who has to write [job] every week. $19.’

Week-by-week

Week 1: 25 prompts and three proofs. Week 2: listing live, ten personal shares in the niche. Week 3: add the SOP. Week 4: a bundle with the calendar or checklist if they ask for it.

Mistakes

Clever prompts that do not match how the job is actually done. Medical, legal, or financial claims. Copying someone else’s stack and swapping the niche word.

The job of a video

Every video does one of three things: teach a small piece, diagnose a mistake, or send them to the next step. If it does none of those, it is entertainment you are not getting paid for.

AI is useful for hooks and outlines. It is poison if you read it like a press release. Generate ten hooks. Speak the three you would actually say to a friend. Cut the rest.

The first 72 hours

Pick one platform. TikTok, Instagram, or YouTube Shorts — not all three. Write the one-line offer that every video can point to.

Generate 20 hooks. Film three 20-second videos: a mistake, a principle, a walkthrough. Same offer at the end. Same link in the bio.

Publish one a day starting today. Batch the next five on day three. That is the 72 hours. The algorithm is not the bottleneck. Volume of honest posts is.

The path, step by step

Step 1. One offer in the bio. Not a menu.

Step 2. A hook formula: ‘Stop doing [common thing] if you want [result].’ Then show the better way in 15 seconds.

Step 3. On-screen text for people who watch muted. Your face or your screen — pick one style and keep it for 30 days.

Step 4. Batch. Two hours, ten videos, beats ten days of ‘I should post.’

Step 5. Read comments. The objections are next week’s scripts. Paste them into AI and ask for outlines, then rewrite.

Step 6. Pin a comment with the next step if the platform allows it.

Step 7. After 30 videos, keep the 5 that got saves and comments. Repeat those shapes. Kill the rest.

Copy

Hook: ‘Stop starting from a blank chat if you need [result] this week.’

End card: ‘The file is $19. Link in bio. One job, not a catalog.’

Week-by-week

Week 1: 7 videos, same offer. Week 2: 7 more, comments become scripts. Week 3: repeat winners. Week 4: one live or one longer walkthrough pointing at the same checkout.

Mistakes

Posting with no offer. Switching platforms when a post dies. Using a voice you would not use in a kitchen. Buying followers. Explaining your whole catalog in 20 seconds.

Write issue one by hand, then optionally run it through AI to tighten — not to inflate. Build the form. The free file is a one-page scorecard or a 7-day checklist, useful even if they never buy.

Step 1. Promise one specific weekly note. Keep it. Same day, same length.

Step 2. Subject lines that are specific: ‘The 20-minute follow-up I actually send,’ not ‘Newsletter #4.’

Step 3. End with one ask: reply, click, or try the prompt. Not three.

Step 4. Once a month, a quieter issue that points to the paid file.

Step 5. Never buy a list. Never scrape. Your deliverability is the business.

Copy
